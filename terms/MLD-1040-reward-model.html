Language-model training and adaptation · MLD-1040

Reward model

A learned model assigning scores intended to approximate desired outcomes or preferences.

Expanded guide · editorial review 2026-11-02. Original definitions, examples, notes and citations are preserved below. New examples are illustrative, not benchmark results.

Prerequisites

  • GRPO
  • Process reward model
  • Reward hacking
  • Goodhart's law

What a learned reward model estimates

A reward model assigns a scalar or preference score to an action, trajectory or generated answer. In preference-based language-model training, a scorer is fit to pairs of candidate responses labeled by people. Its output is an estimate of those comparisons under the sampling protocol, not a direct measurement of truth, human welfare or an agent's objective.

From pairwise judgments to a score

For a prompt x and answers y_good and y_bad, one common model estimates P(y_good preferred) = sigmoid(r(x,y_good) - r(x,y_bad)). Fitting pairwise cross-entropy encourages the preferred answer to have the higher score. Absolute score offsets cancel from this probability; score magnitudes across separately trained models are therefore not automatically comparable.

How the model enters a training loop

A typical RLHF workflow collects prompts and two or more candidate responses, obtains comparisons, fits a reward scorer, then improves a policy against that frozen or periodically updated scorer. PPO can optimize the policy while penalizing divergence from a reference model; best-of-N instead samples several candidates and selects the highest-scoring one. These are distinct selection pressures with different costs and failure modes.

Worked miniature preference example

Suppose a learned scorer gives response A a score of 1.2 and response B a score of 0.4 on the same prompt. The difference is 0.8, so the logistic pairwise model assigns P(A preferred over B) = 1/(1+exp(-0.8)) ≈ 0.69. This arithmetic is illustrative, not a calibrated human-preference probability on an unseen prompt: real confidence depends on dataset, label noise and distribution.

When reward modeling is useful

Preference scorers can provide dense feedback where a direct task reward is missing, such as summarization helpfulness, style or answer quality. They can also rank candidate outputs or guide offline evaluation. Their usefulness is conditional on the comparison dataset covering intended behavior and on evaluation that does not recycle the model's own training labels.

A practical reward-model audit

Record annotator instructions, population and agreement; split data by source/task to prevent train-test leakage; retain untouched held-out pairs; examine performance on ambiguous, adversarial and out-of-distribution examples. Report pairwise accuracy by slice, calibration or score-rank reliability, refusal bias and correlations with independent human judgments. RewardBench supplies research examples of challenging ranking tests, not a universal certificate.

Threats from optimization and Goodhart effects

When a policy selects outputs to maximize a learned score, it shifts its output distribution toward regions where the scorer is high. The scorer can reward verbosity, formatting or other shortcuts that correlate with judged quality in training data. A rising reward score with falling independently assessed quality is evidence of proxy exploitation in that experiment; it does not by itself establish intentional deception by the policy.

Operational guardrails and comparison baselines

Keep an independently evaluated baseline, vary the strength of policy optimization or best-of-N, and plot both reward-model score and held-out human/task quality. Audit high-score outliers and use separate raters, deterministic task checkers or real environment outcomes where possible. KL penalties, diverse preference data and refreshed evaluation can help, but no single knob guarantees that an imperfect learned proxy remains aligned.

Evidence boundaries and common misconceptions

A reward model is not the same as the policy being optimized, and a pairwise preference probability is not a validated probability of correctness. A good benchmark rank does not prove the scorer robust to adaptive optimization, unseen domains or changes in reviewer values. Christiano et al. studied preference-defined RL, Stiennon et al. studied human-feedback summarization, and RewardBench provides controlled reward-model tests; these findings should not be reported as production-wide safety outcomes.
