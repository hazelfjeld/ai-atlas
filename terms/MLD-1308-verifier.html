Verifier

A system checking candidate outputs or intermediate steps against a criterion.

Expanded guide · editorial review 2026-11-02. Original definitions, examples, notes and citations are preserved below. New examples are illustrative, not benchmark results.

Prerequisites

  • RLVR
  • Process reward model
  • Agent evaluation
  • Reward hacking
  • Train-test contamination

What a verifier actually verifies

A verifier checks a candidate answer, trajectory or intermediate step against a particular criterion. Some verifiers are deterministic programs such as compilers, unit tests or formal proof checkers; others are learned models that predict correctness from examples. Its output describes whether a specified check was passed, not whether the entire task is safe or a model's internal objective is aligned. Always write down the verifier's exact input and acceptance contract.

Separate hard constraints from learned rankings

For code, a sandboxed test runner can reject solutions that fail public cases but cannot prove correctness on unspecified inputs. A learned outcome verifier scores a whole answer, while a process reward model scores intermediate reasoning steps under a labeling scheme. Combining deterministic validity checks with a trained ranker can be useful, but neither category should quietly become an authority on facts outside its tested scope.

Walk through a synthetic candidate-selection example

Suppose a toy task has four independently generated answers. A learned verifier assigns scores 0.80, 0.70, 0.60 and 0.40, but an independently checked answer key finds that only the second and fourth are correct. Selecting the highest score therefore chooses a wrong answer. With a deterministic independent evaluator, verified success is two of four candidates, and the top-ranked candidate is a false positive. These scores are invented teaching data, not published benchmark results.

Show why best-of-N amplifies verifier weaknesses

In best-of-N selection, when generating N alternatives and picking argmax_i V(answer_i), the system optimizes the verifier's score, not necessarily the true objective. As N increases, candidates may exploit ranking errors, benchmark contamination or fragile heuristics. Recompute true correctness using an independent sealed evaluator as the sampling budget grows; plot selected-answer accuracy against N and the rate of invalid high-scoring answers. More candidates do not guarantee safer outputs.

Understand outcome versus process supervision

Cobbe and colleagues studied learned verifiers that rank generated solutions for GSM8K math questions. Uesato and colleagues contrasted outcome-level and step-level feedback and observed differences between final-answer performance and reasoning errors. Lightman and colleagues found substantial benefits for process supervision on the MATH benchmark with their experimental models and data. These are results in defined math-task settings, not universal proof that a process verifier captures honest reasoning or hidden intent.

Threat-model the verification boundary

Record which inputs an adversarial candidate can control, whether the checker receives untrusted instructions, which files or services it can access, and which side effects are prohibited. Treat retrieved documents, generated tests and model-authored explanations as data rather than authority to redefine success. Run code candidates with network disabled, minimal permissions, resource limits and non-secret fixtures; never allow a candidate to edit the evaluation harness or its own hidden answer key.

Design a bounded reproducible verifier audit

Proposed test, not executed: create twenty synthetic tasks with known answers, four candidate responses each and deliberately planted arithmetic, dependency and format errors. Freeze a deterministic checker and a separately trained ranking verifier. Measure top-1 precision, false-positive rate, false-negative rate and selected-answer correctness under best-of-1, best-of-2 and best-of-4 budgets. Repeat across independent seeds and compare with random selection and answer-only baselines on a sealed set.

Calibrate errors and test adversarial robustness

A verifier score is not automatically a calibrated probability. Evaluate reliability diagrams and Brier or log-loss metrics only when the output has a probabilistic interpretation and the labels are independently reliable. Test near-duplicates, paraphrases, contaminated benchmarks, unusual formats and misleading chain-of-thought explanations. If a small subset receives most errors, show the sample sizes and conditions instead of reporting only pooled ranking accuracy.

Mitigate incentives to game the checker

Use outcome checks that inspect final environment state, independent hidden tests, human review for unresolved policy claims and separation of generator and evaluator permissions. Version the verifier, tests and rubric; track failure clusters and retain counterexamples as regression cases. Avoid training repeatedly on a public fixed suite without a sealed holdout. Reject or quarantine invalid candidates even when their learned score is high; document which failures remain untestable.

Keep the evidence and safety distinction explicit

Verifier research demonstrates that ranking or step-level supervision can improve measured problem-solving under specific experimental conditions. It does not establish that an agent is truthful, that an external action is authorized, or that no reward hacking will occur. A verifier can be incomplete, miscalibrated or itself exploitable. This guide's toy arithmetic and proposed audit are illustrative, not newly observed security incidents or an evaluation of any deployed product.
