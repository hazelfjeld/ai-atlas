Concept drift

A change over time in the relationship between inputs and targets.

Expanded guide · editorial review 2026-11-03. Original definitions, examples, notes and citations are preserved below. New examples are illustrative, not benchmark results.

Prerequisites

  • Distribution shift
  • Data drift
  • Covariate shift
  • Calibration

Mechanism and what actually changes

Concept drift is a time-dependent change in the conditional target relationship P(Y|X,t). A stable input-feature distribution does not rule it out: the same measured X may begin predicting a different Y when users, policies, incentives or the environment change. Unlike ordinary data drift, concept drift is a change in the predictive relationship rather than merely a shift in input frequency.

Concrete example: identical inputs, changed labels

Imagine a synthetic fraud detector for which transactions in one fixed feature bucket were fraudulent with probability 0.20 in a past period and 0.70 in a later period. The measured X bucket and its frequency can stay the same while the conditional label probability changes. An unchanged thresholding rule that predicts 'not fraud' in this bucket changes from about 80% to 30% expected accuracy; this toy result is not a measured bank outcome.

A worked loss calculation

For a binary label, a constant probability forecast p has Brier loss (p-y)^2. Before drift, the correct 0.20 forecast has expected loss 0.20(0.80^2)+0.80(0.20^2)=0.16. After the conditional probability becomes 0.70, retaining p=0.20 gives 0.70(0.80^2)+0.30(0.20^2)=0.46, whereas a correctly updated p=0.70 gives 0.70(0.30^2)+0.30(0.70^2)=0.21. The illustrative regret is 0.25, not a guaranteed real-world improvement.

Separating related shifts

Data drift tests whether P(X) moved, label shift asks whether P(Y) changed while class-conditional inputs may remain stable, and concept drift is defined here by a changed P(Y|X). These mechanisms may co-occur. A decline in predictive accuracy alone cannot diagnose which mechanism caused it; deployment bugs, missing features, delayed labeling and selection effects can look similar.

How to detect it without leaking future data

Keep the model and its threshold frozen while evaluating each time-ordered, independently labeled slice. Log schema/version, prediction timestamp, label arrival time and ground-truth provenance. Use rolling proper scores and class-conditional error rates, then compare against a predeclared baseline with uncertainty intervals; account for seasonality and delayed or censored labels. A change detector can flag a candidate, not certify its cause.

Prequential evaluation in a controlled experiment

For streaming evaluation, predict each newly arriving labeled example before using its label for an update: test-then-train, never train-then-test. Compare a frozen baseline, periodic retraining and drift-triggered adaptation under equal data latency, compute, tuning budgets and review costs. Report metrics over time including immediately after a change point, not just a single aggregate score.

Adaptation and intervention choices

If a shift is confirmed, consider recalibrating probabilities, updating features, retraining on a recent labeled window, weighting examples or routing to an audited specialist model. None is automatically best: a short window may forget rare historical cases, and uncontrolled online learning can absorb attack or label errors. Use shadow tests, rollback conditions and independent post-deployment monitoring before switching traffic.

Reliability and AI-safety applications

Concept drift matters for spam and abuse classifiers, medical risk estimates, fraud screening and learned reward or policy-evaluation systems whose feedback criteria may change. If an AI agent seems to optimize a new proxy, first distinguish a changing task or label definition from specification gaming or strategic misbehavior; drift detection alone is not evidence of deceptive alignment or an actual incident.

Limits, confounders and reproducibility

Without representative and timely labels, a test of inputs or predicted probabilities does not establish changed P(Y|X). Logging changes, subgroup mix, selective labels, repeated hypothesis testing and temporal leakage can create false alarms. Publish the label definitions, time windows, missingness policy, detection threshold, detector false-alert rate, seeds, update rule and before/after metrics; a controlled demonstration is not proof of field reliability.
