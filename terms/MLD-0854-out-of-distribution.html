Out-of-distribution

Inputs outside or substantially different from a model's reference data distribution.

Expanded guide · editorial review 2026-11-02. Original definitions, examples, notes and citations are preserved below. New examples are illustrative, not benchmark results.

  • Generalization

What out-of-distribution means

An input is out-of-distribution (OOD) relative to a stated training or reference distribution, not intrinsically OOD in every possible context. A new sensor, rare subgroup or novel semantic class can produce different kinds of shift; distinguishing near-OOD from far-OOD requires an explicitly documented reference and evaluation population.

Input shift is not an error label

An OOD flag describes departure from a reference data distribution, whereas misclassification is a comparison with a known label. A detector can correctly flag a novel input that the classifier would have labeled correctly, or miss a familiar-looking input that the classifier gets wrong. Neither score alone proves an unsafe action occurred.

Worked deployment scenario

Consider a vision model evaluated on camera images from one warehouse, then presented with infrared camera images or novel package types. Make separate held-out sets for known packages under lighting shift, unseen package types, and ordinary noisy images. A detector that handles infrared inputs may still miss semantic novelties in familiar colors.

A simple detection baseline

Given class probabilities p(y|x), maximum-softmax-probability detection uses s(x)=1-max_y p(y|x); a larger value suggests uncertainty. For invented predictions [0.95,0.03,0.02] and [0.42,0.38,0.20], the scores are 0.05 and 0.58. This ordering is illustrative, not proof that a model's confidence is calibrated or OOD-safe.

Choosing a threshold without leakage

Select a threshold on a separate development set using explicitly permitted proxy OOD data, then freeze it before evaluating untouched near- and far-OOD test sets. Label which OOD types were visible during tuning. Testing repeatedly on the final holdout and adjusting the threshold would turn an external evaluation into model selection.

Metrics that expose operational costs

Report AUROC and AUPR alongside FPR at 95% true-positive rate (FPR95), false rejections on in-distribution data, stratified recall and coverage-risk curves. With a 1% prevalence of truly anomalous inputs, even a detector with 90% true-positive rate and 5% false-positive rate produces around 9 true alerts and 49.5 false alerts per 1,000 inputs; these numbers are synthetic expectations.

Alternative scores and safeguards

ODIN perturbs inputs and temperature-scales softmax scores; feature-space distances, energy scores and ensembles provide other approaches. Each has dataset, preprocessing and compute assumptions. Separate detection from the policy for abstention, fallback or human review, and verify that routing decisions are authorized and measurable.

Limits of reliability claims

A detector trained on one benchmark may fail on unknown shifts, adversarially chosen data or semantically close novelties. Strong AUROC on a selected collection is not evidence of universal anomaly detection, real-world incident prevention or calibrated error probabilities. Evaluate confidence calibration, classifier correctness and detector utility separately.

Reproducible research checklist

Publish the in-distribution reference definition, dates, dataset provenance, near/far OOD sources, inclusion rules, duplicate checks, model/seed settings, threshold-selection protocol, and a confusion matrix at a fixed operating point. Repeat across different target domains and report where performance breaks; do not claim independent replication unless performed.
