Data and feature engineering · MLD-0263

Data leakage

Use of information during training or selection that would not be available for the intended prediction.

Expanded guide · editorial review 2026-11-02. Original definitions, examples, notes and citations are preserved below. New examples are illustrative, not benchmark results.

Prerequisites

  • Test set
  • Group split
  • Target leakage
  • Overfitting

The prediction-time information boundary

Data leakage means that fitting, selecting or assessing a model used information unavailable under the intended prediction setting. The boundary includes not just labels but timestamps, duplicate rows, correlated groups, metadata and preprocessing statistics. A high reported test score cannot repair a broken information boundary.

Learn transformations only from the training side

Split observations before calculating vocabulary, normalization means, imputation values, feature-selection scores or PCA components. In cross-validation, fit every learned transformation on the training fold and apply that frozen transformation to validation data. A scikit-learn Pipeline helps enforce this separation.

Illustrative normalization arithmetic

Imagine training values 8 and 12 with mean 10, and a future test value of 100. Centering the training examples with mean 10 is legitimate; recomputing the mean from all three values yields 40 and allows the future test observation to affect learned training features. This arithmetic is illustrative and is not a measured accuracy improvement.

Duplicate, patient and document overlap

A random row split can leak when different rows are near-duplicates or concern the same patient, customer, document or event. Use group-aware splits when the goal is prediction for unseen groups, and define the independent evaluation unit before splitting. Near-duplicate prompts and copied documents can make held-out evaluation much less independent than it appears.

Temporal and target leakage

A final diagnosis, post-outcome billing code or later moderation action cannot support a prediction meant to occur before that information exists. Choose the prediction time and audit the availability timestamp for every feature. Evaluate forecasts on later periods when appropriate rather than shuffling across a future-information boundary.

Tuning and model selection also leak

Feature screening, prompt revision, threshold selection and hyperparameter tuning based on final test labels turn that test set into part of the decision process, even if gradient descent never saw it. Use development validation or nested cross-validation for choices, reserve a sealed final holdout and document repeated benchmark feedback.

A practical audit before publishing

For each feature, record the source, collection time, availability at inference and relationship to outcomes. Search for duplicate IDs, shared authors, templates, copied prompts and model-training contamination. Rebuild preprocessing and hyperparameter tuning within each fold, then compare with a simple baseline and report uncertainty.

Leakage in language-model and safety evaluations

Benchmark prompts may appear in training examples, synthetic fine-tuning data or repeated evaluator feedback. Partition by source, time or template where defensible, test newly authored held-out tasks and prevent the grader from revealing expected answers. Contamination undermines evaluation independence but does not itself prove deliberate reward hacking.

How to interpret a corrected score

If measured accuracy falls after removing leakage, the original score was biased for the intended deployment boundary. The corrected estimate still needs uncertainty intervals, distribution-shift assessment and representative test data. Kapoor and Narayanan document scientific ML reproducibility pitfalls; their observations are not a universal prevalence estimate for every study.
